# V5 (T3-2): Why the BOM and the spend cube don't tie

Asked: 2026-11-13 ~11:26 PM CT

## Prompt (sent by Nathaniel as worded in the thread)
What are the specific reasons a manufacturer's BOM part numbers do not match its purchasing and spend records? Cover part number differences across plants and systems, supplier part numbers versus internal numbers, revisions and supersessions, units of measure, make versus buy items, kits and phantom assemblies, spare and warranty parts bought outside production, and free-text or uncoded spend. For each, say how analysts resolve it today. Label public sources versus inference.

## Velocity response (verbatim; the table was tab-separated in the paste and is reformatted as a markdown table, wording unchanged)
Received: 2026-11-13 11:28 PM CT

A BOM part number often identifies what engineering specifies, while a purchasing record identifies what a particular plant ordered or was invoiced for. Those are not always the same object—or even at the same level of detail. In the two-plant scenario, analysts therefore build an auditable crosswalk rather than treating a failed exact-number join as proof that the part was never bought.

Source boundary: The cited public sources document how BOMs, purchasing records and service parts can be represented in common enterprise systems. The "how analysts resolve it today" column is my inference about a practical engagement workflow, not a published Kearney PERLab procedure.

| Why the numbers do not tie | Public-source basis | How analysts resolve it today — inference |
|---|---|---|
| Different numbers across plants and systems. Engineering/PLM may use one ID, while each plant's ERP has its own material ID. Even identical-looking numbers must be qualified by source system and plant. | SAP describes a material number as an identifier in the SAP system, and BOM information includes plant. Neither establishes an enterprise-wide identity across two ERP instances. [SAP material number · SAP BOM fields] | Keep (system, plant, item ID) as the original key. Build a separate canonical-part crosswalk using existing migration tables, drawings/specifications, manufacturer IDs and buyer/engineering review. Do not merge on description alone: similar descriptions can conceal different tolerances or approvals. |
| Supplier number versus internal number. A PO or invoice may show a supplier's catalog SKU or manufacturer part number rather than the BOM's internal ID; a distributor's SKU and the actual manufacturer part number may differ again. | SAP purchasing-info records relate a material to a vendor and can contain a supplier material number; SAP also distinguishes manufacturer part numbers on POs. [SAP purchasing-info record · SAP manufacturer-part documentation] | Join through purchasing-info records, approved-manufacturer lists, supplier catalogs and PO-line references. Record which supplier and manufacturer each number belongs to; ask a buyer to verify ambiguous or repackaged SKUs. |
| Revisions, alternates and supersessions. The BOM may specify a newer revision while historical POs carry an older item number—or a successor may replace several older service parts. A change in drawing revision does not always create a new purchasing ID. | BOM validity can differ at header and item level, while service-parts replacement strategies can be one-to-one, one-to-many or many-to-one. [SAP BOM validity · SAP service-parts replacement strategies] | Make the crosswalk date- and direction-aware: preserve old ID, successor ID, revision, effective date and approved substitution rule. Compare the BOM effective for the build with purchases from the relevant period; have engineering confirm fit and interchangeability rather than collapsing every revision into one part. |
| Units of measure and price basis. A BOM may call for 4 each; a plant buys one pack of 10, while an invoice quotes a price per 100. The part might match but its quantities and unit prices will appear inconsistent. | SAP explicitly supports conversion between base and purchasing units, including material-dependent conversions. [SAP unit conversion · SAP order-unit example] | Retain the original quantity, unit and price basis; use the item-specific conversion factor to calculate comparable per-BOM-unit quantities and prices. Flag missing or conflicting conversions—especially variable-length material, weight-based purchases and kits—for manual checks. |
| Make versus buy. An engineering BOM lists an in-house subassembly, but purchasing buys its components; alternatively, a plant buys a complete assembly whose engineering BOM lists its children. The BOM header and PO line are at different levels. | SAP distinguishes planning scopes for assemblies and purchased parts. [SAP engineering-to-material BOM documentation] | Apply plant- and date-specific procurement status. For a made assembly, explode to the relevant purchased children; for a bought assembly, match the purchased parent and retain its children only for design analysis. Do not count both parent purchase price and child purchases as the same product cost. |
| Kits and phantom assemblies. A supplier may sell one kit SKU covering several BOM components. Conversely, a phantom BOM header organizes children but is not itself stocked or purchased. | SAP describes a phantom assembly as a structure whose components flow into the parent without a goods movement for the phantom; kits can have their own BOM and may be made or procured externally. [SAP phantom assembly · SAP kit-to-stock] | Identify whether the transaction is for a purchased kit, internally assembled kit, or phantom. Obtain kit contents and effective quantities; match spend at the purchased kit level, allocating it to components only when a defensible allocation is needed. Explode phantoms to real purchased children; do not expect a phantom PO. |
| Spare and warranty parts outside production. A replacement part might have a service SKU, come in a service kit, or be a successor compatible with several machine models. Its purchase may appear in a service warehouse's spend, not either production plant's purchases. | SAP supports separate service BOM usage and service-parts substitution relationships; those structures need not be the same as a production BOM. [SAP service BOM example · SAP service-parts replacement strategies] | Obtain service catalogs, service-to-production cross-references, kit contents and warranty claim/work-order consumption. Link to the product family by model/serial applicability, but report production, aftermarket spare and warranty demand separately so a replacement purchase is not presented as another component in every newly built machine. |
| Free-text or uncoded spend. A noncatalog PO may have a description but no item ID; an invoice may be coded to a category or account rather than a BOM part. Some descriptions simply lack enough detail to identify a component. | Oracle allows PO lines with no item number, using a purchasing category and description instead. [Oracle purchase-order lines] | First follow the invoice back to its PO line, requisition, receipt or supplier item reference. Then use supplier, description, specification, price/unit and order history to generate candidate matches for buyer review. Classify genuinely indeterminate lines as unresolved spend; report their value rather than forcing a false BOM match. |

The practical rule: separate identity matching ("is this the same approved physical part?") from cost attribution ("was this purchase for production, a kit, a spare, or a warranty repair?"). One canonical part can legitimately have several plant, supplier and service numbers—and not every purchase of that part should be charged to the production BOM.
