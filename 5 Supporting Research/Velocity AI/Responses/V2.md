The decision is not "is this part used on many machines?" It is: can it remain unchanged across the target machines without sacrificing required performance, while putting customer-valued differences into controlled, interchangeable choices? Kearney PERLab describes the three levers distinctly: a platform is a stable technical foundation, modularity creates controlled variety on it, and standardization removes unnecessary variation. Kearney also warns that forcing one design across unlike duties can produce over-specified, costly machines. Those are public-source principles, not a published PERLab component-scoring algorithm. [Sources cited: Kearney PERLab architecture series · Kearney industrial modularization approach]

### How I would make the three-way decision

The criteria in this table are a practical synthesis/inference, informed by the cited public approaches—not a claim that PERLab uses this exact checklist.

| Test | Common core | Configurable module | Product-specific / unique |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customer requirement | Same underlying function and adequate performance envelope across target applications. | A meaningful requirement varies predictably—say throughput, voltage, hazardous-area rating, or control capability—and can be served by a limited set of pre-engineered choices. | A requirement is genuinely exceptional, or an order needs engineering outside the approved choices. |
| Physical and software interfaces | Can retain a stable design and connection points. | Variants can exchange at a governed interface without redesigning the rest of the machine. | Changes propagate through multiple subsystems; a purported "option" requires bespoke integration. |
| Cost of sharing | Reuse savings outweigh extra material, capacity, weight, certification, or redesign cost imposed on simpler models. | Sharing the interface/core pays off, but making the whole assembly identical would over-specify lower-duty machines. | Standardizing would impose unacceptable performance, safety, cost, or customer-value penalties. |
| Demand and life cycle | Used broadly enough—or strategically important enough—to justify common engineering, sourcing and maintenance. | Distinct choices recur often enough to justify designing, validating and maintaining a small variant library. | Truly rare, customer-specific, or likely to change independently; manage as a controlled exception rather than silently adding it to the standard catalog. |

This follows Kearney's public sequence: establish priority segments and specifications; distinguish inputs that determine basic configuration from lesser variations; define product families and a variant tree; then optimize without over-standardizing. Kearney evaluates solutions using cost, maintenance cost, time to market and market coverage—not maximum part sharing in isolation. [Kearney industrial modularization approach]

Machinery example (illustrative inference): A frame and control-network interface might be core if they meet every intended duty without excessive overdesign. Drive packages of several validated power ratings might be configurable modules behind that interface. A one-off, unusually corrosive process-contact assembly might remain unique until repeat demand and engineering evidence justify a qualified module. The labels are architecture decisions, not intrinsic properties of a part number.

### Indices and metrics: what each actually tells you

Before calculating any index, fix the product-family boundary, BOM level, revision/effectivity, definition of an "identical" part, and whether you count a component once per model or by quantity. Otherwise a change in the score may reflect counting conventions rather than better architecture. A published study of the Product Line Commonality Index found material variation from different disassembly levels, omitted parts and inconsistent judgments of sameness. [Thevenot and Simpson, Design Studies]

#### Named measures from public literature

| Measure | Calculation | Best use—and limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Degree of Commonality Index (DCI) | DCI = Σⱼ φⱼ / d, where d is the number of distinct component types and φⱼ is the number of immediate parent items using component j, measured at a consistently chosen product-structure level. | Public literature: Average parent uses per distinct component. Easy BOM-based baseline, but it has no universal fixed upper bound and does not by itself account for demand, quantities used, cost or whether sharing is desirable. Do not treat a higher DCI as proof of a better platform. [Wazed et al., commonality review] |
| Product Line Commonality Index (PCI) | For each eligible, non-differentiating component category i, let N = number of products, nᵢ = number containing that category, and f₁ᵢ, f₂ᵢ, f₃ᵢ = respectively the largest identical group divided by nᵢ for size/shape, material/manufacturing process, and assembly/fastening. Set CCIᵢ = nᵢf₁ᵢf₂ᵢf₃ᵢ. Then PCI = 100 × [ΣᵢCCIᵢ − Σᵢ(1/nᵢ²)] / [PN − Σᵢ(1/nᵢ²)], with P eligible categories. | Public literature: A 0–100 family score that targets differences in components judged capable of standardization, rather than penalizing every intentionally distinctive feature. It demands engineering judgment on eligibility and "identical" attributes; coding those judgments consistently is essential. [Thevenot and Simpson, equation and definitions] |
| Generational Variety Index (GVI) and Coupling Index (CI) | In the published Design for Variety method, GVI scores a component's expected redesign exposure to changing market/engineering requirements; coupling is derived from a component-dependency matrix, with row and column totals indicating influence received and supplied. Do not confuse this CI with an index named "Commonality Index." | Public literature: Useful architecture diagnostics rather than simple BOM-sharing counts. A frequently changing, strongly coupled subsystem is a warning against casually placing it inside a supposedly stable core. Exact scoring depends on the method's requirement ratings and dependency matrix, so I would specify those scales before reporting numbers. [Martin and Ishii method · Application explaining coupling row/column scores] |

#### Operational measures I would add (my inference, not standardized PERLab indices)

These make the named indices actionable at the component or assembly level. They are defined here so two teams can calculate them the same way; they should not be presented as universally named standards.

- Model reuse coverage: number of in-scope models containing the exact approved part ÷ number of in-scope models. Also report unit-weighted coverage: units shipped containing it ÷ total relevant units shipped. The first shows architectural breadth; the second prevents a rarely sold model from carrying the same weight as a high-volume one.
- Part-number proliferation for a function: distinct active, non-interchangeable part numbers serving the function, reported alongside the number of distinct validated performance needs. Five motor numbers for five real duty bands differ from five near-identical motors created by historical projects.
- Variant-tree market coverage: historical orders (or forecast demand) that approved core + module combinations can meet without new engineering ÷ all in-scope orders (or demand). Track exception rate as the complement, while separating truly novel requests from bad option coding. Kearney publicly identifies market coverage and reduction in solutions/components as important variant-tree measures; this particular operational formula is my inference. [Kearney industrial modularization approach]
- Interface-change burden: for a proposed module, count the other assemblies, control elements, drawings, tests or certifications that must change when its variant changes. A low count supports a module boundary; a high count signals hidden coupling. This is my diagnostic, not a published standard index.
- Net platform economics: avoided engineering + tooling + purchasing/inventory + assembly/service costs − incremental common-part over-specification − interface/module development and qualification − ongoing governance costs, assessed over an agreed life cycle. This is a decision model, not a single standard commonality index. It guards against raising DCI or PCI while making a low-end machine more expensive. Kearney's public discussion explicitly cautions against over-specified standardized designs. [Kearney industrial modularization approach]

Bottom line: Use DCI for a quick, carefully scoped picture of existing reuse; PCI to examine avoidable differences among parts that should plausibly be shared; and change/coupling analysis plus order coverage and economics to decide where sharing belongs. None of those family-level indices can, by itself, decree that a particular gearbox, frame or controls assembly should be core, modular or unique.
