There is no single tool that automatically turns an engineering BOM into a trustworthy answer to "what did we pay for this product?" In the two-plant example, teams typically combine ERP transaction data, engineering structures, a part crosswalk and human review. Software can accelerate candidate matching and cost rollups; it cannot, by itself, establish that two nearly identical parts are approved substitutes or that a purchase was consumed by a particular machine.

Source boundary: Public below describes documented tool capabilities and observed data problems. Inference identifies the practical limitation or workflow I would expect in this engagement; it is not a claim about Kearney PERLab's undisclosed tool stack.

| Tool or method used today | What it contributes — public source | Biggest limitation for this manufacturer — inference |
|---|---|---|
| ERP reports and BI extracts | Material masters, purchasing-info records, PO/invoice lines, material prices and production-order consumption provide the transaction and valuation evidence. SAP's purchasing-info records, for example, are material–supplier records that can be plant-specific. [SAP purchasing-info records] | Each ERP understands its own IDs and postings. An ERP report from Plant A will not establish equivalence to Plant B's part number, reconcile an engineering revision, or recover a missing item ID on a free-text line. Reports must also distinguish purchases from inventory issues and service consumption. |
| Spend-analytics software | Platforms such as Sievo describe ingesting invoices, POs, material and vendor masters, purchasing-info records and BOMs across ERPs; they harmonize suppliers/materials and normalize currency and units for price comparisons and BOM-linked analysis. [Sievo direct procurement] | A classified spend line is not necessarily linked to an engineering-approved BOM part, and a harmonized material cluster is not necessarily proof of interchangeability. The useful output is often a prioritized set of matches and anomalies requiring validation. |
| PLM and product-cost modules | SAP Ariba Product Sourcing supports BOM-based price collection and cost rollups. Siemens describes Teamcenter Product Cost Management using BOM and ERP cost data for product calculations and quotations; its integration gateway is intended to reuse ERP materials and prices. [SAP Ariba Product Sourcing · Siemens Teamcenter gateway] | These are strong for designed or modeled cost, but a costed engineering BOM is not automatically a reconciliation to all historical invoices, inventory issues, spares and warranty claims. | Analyst spreadsheets, SQL and ETL scripts | This is a practical bridge for joining extracts, preserving source-line keys, normalizing units and creating a reviewed mapping table. Public accounts note that ERP/BI data can require specialist extraction skills and that relevant information also resides in spreadsheets and specification documents. [McKinsey on technology-enabled procurement] | Inference: Flexible and fast for one family, but manual mappings are easy to break when a BOM revision, supplier SKU or ERP extract changes. Without version control, match reasons and review ownership, a spreadsheet becomes an unmaintainable second part master. |
| Fuzzy matching and technical similarity search | Text analytics can tokenize descriptions and propose exact or partial matches for category-manager validation; other approaches combine spend with specifications or CAD-derived attributes to identify similar parts. [McKinsey on digital MRO procurement · McKinsey on product analytics] | Inference: Similar text, dimensions or shape make a part a candidate, not a substitute. Material grade, tolerance, certification, interface, revision or customer approval can invalidate a seemingly excellent match. Engineering must approve consequential equivalences. |
| AI classification and material harmonization | Tools describe using AI to cleanse, categorize and identify duplicate or comparable materials across fragmented ERPs; McKinsey says manufacturers can start with imperfect descriptions and purchase history, improving quality iteratively. [McKinsey on production spend · Sievo direct procurement] | Inference: AI can reduce the search space, but it cannot reconstruct a missing drawing specification or reliably decide whether a free-text invoice describes a production component, a repair kit or a service. Confidence scores and a review queue matter more than an impressive raw match count. |

### Why it remains painstaking

The difficult work is not primarily joining two tables. It is resolving several different questions that happen to look like one join:

- Which object is this? Engineering ID, two plant IDs, supplier SKU, manufacturer part number and service SKU may name the same physical item—or subtly different ones.
- Which version and purchasing level? The effective BOM may list an assembly while the PO buys children, a kit or a successor revision.
- What is the comparable price? Unit conversions, currency, freight, quantity breaks and dates must be normalized before a price spread is meaningful.
- Where was it consumed? A PO shows a purchase; it does not prove which build used the item. Inventory, production, spare sales and warranty repair are distinct destinations.
- Who can approve the answer? Procurement can confirm a supplier SKU and finance can confirm booked value, but engineering may need to approve technical equivalence and service may need to confirm machine applicability.

Public evidence for the underlying fragmentation: McKinsey describes manufacturing spend, supplier and material data spread across ERPs, plants and material masters, with specifications also embedded in unstructured descriptions. It notes that perfect data is not required to begin, but improvement should be iterative. [McKinsey on production spend]

My inference: The most durable output is therefore not a one-time "matched" spreadsheet. It is a refreshable bridge from source BOM line → plant item → approved physical part or kit → purchasing line → consumption destination, retaining effective dates, original IDs, match method, reviewer and unresolved exceptions. Automation handles repeatable joins; people resolve the high-value cases where the records do not contain enough information—or where a wrong match could lead to a bad sourcing or design decision.
