## Velocity response, round 2 (verbatim; the table was tab-separated in the paste and is reformatted as a markdown table, wording unchanged)

For an industrial-machinery manufacturer, the first month is usually less "design a universal machine" and more make the existing product family legible: connect what customers specify to what engineering designs, what factories build, and what the company actually sells. The first credible platform hypothesis is then a distinction among a common core, controlled module choices, and genuinely necessary unique engineering.

Evidence boundary: Kearney publicly describes a market-driven modularization approach—prioritize segments, translate customer specifications, define and optimize product families and a variant tree, then create module libraries and governance. It does not publish a standard PERLab week-by-week staffing plan, client data-request list, file formats, or analyst-hour benchmarks. The sequence, formats, and effort ranges below are my estimates for a scoped machinery product family, not reported Kearney engagement data. Kearney's heat-exchanger platforming example does confirm analysis at component, system, and finished-product levels, tied to customer applications. Effort interpretation: Those rows total roughly 21–39 analyst-days for one bounded family over four calendar weeks, spread across analysts and overlapping activities—not 21–39 elapsed days. They exclude substantial client extraction time, senior engineering validation, and any detailed redesign. A multi-plant or heavily engineer-to-order family could exceed that range.

Where the hours go before the platform insight

- Expanding and comparing real BOMs: ~5–9 analyst-days. A headline count of parts is easy; establishing which parts occur in which as-built configurations, at which revision and quantity, is not.
- Joining systems and adjudicating exceptions: ~4–7 days. The hard join is customer requirement → quoted option → designed assembly → manufactured machine. Product-configuration practitioners separately identify CRM/CPQ, CAD/PLM and ERP as distinct system views, and note that customer-facing properties do not automatically align with BOM-level module variants. This supports the likely friction, not the day estimate. [Modular Management on end-to-end configuration]
- Coding historical demand into comparable requirements: ~3–6 days. Free-text specifications and one-off quotes must be translated into consistent performance ranges; otherwise a "common core" can inadvertently be sized for every extreme case.
- Function/interface mapping: ~3–6 days. It consumes analyst preparation and engineering review, but it is also where the first real insight starts. A shared part number is not sufficient evidence of a reusable module; interface compatibility and performance envelope matter.

In practical terms, steps 2–4 are the pre-insight data grind. By the end of week 1 the team may have suggestive anecdotes; by weeks 2–3 it should be able to show a traceable example such as: "These apparently different machines use the same base architecture; these drive-and-control packages can be controlled variants; this high-load application needs a distinct structural element." That is a first platform hypothesis, not yet a released module library. Kearney likewise places detailed digital module libraries after family definition and optimization, rather than treating a preliminary variant tree as implementation-ready.
