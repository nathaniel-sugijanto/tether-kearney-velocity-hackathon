Once BOMs are linked to spend, what analyses does a consulting team run to find cost savings? Cover price comparison of the same or equivalent part across suppliers, plants and products, should-cost gaps, volume consolidation, and identifying duplicate or near-duplicate parts. Once the BOM-to-spend bridge exists, the team should look for differences it can explain and act on, not simply rank the largest price gaps. For this two-plant machinery family, I would run four analyses, keeping production purchases separate from spare and warranty demand and retaining the underlying transaction lines.

| Analysis | What the team tests and how it becomes an opportunity | What must be checked before calling it savings |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Same-part price comparison | Group purchases by a verified physical part, then compare normalized unit prices by supplier, plant, period and the products that use it. Look for Plant A paying more than Plant B, different suppliers quoting different prices, and inconsistent terms for the same part. My screening calculation: (higher comparable unit price − achievable unit price) × eligible future quantity. | Confirm identical specification/revision and approved source; convert units and currency; account for freight, duties, rebates, order size, contract dates and supplier capacity. A price gap is a lead, not an automatically transferable saving. Material harmonization and unit/currency normalization are described publicly as prerequisites for valid cross-system price comparisons. [Sievo direct procurement] |
| 2. Equivalent-part and should-cost gaps | For parts with different IDs but potentially equivalent function, compare drawings, material, dimensions, tolerances and performance before benchmarking prices. For high-value custom parts, estimate what a feasible manufacturing process should cost—material, yield, machine time, labor, overhead and delivery—and compare that model with the paid price. My screening calculation: (current comparable price − validated target price) × contestable future volume. | Engineering must approve interchangeability; a should-cost model needs credible process and input assumptions. The gap may suggest negotiation, redesign or an RFQ, but it is not realized savings. Public should-cost guidance describes bottom-up modeling from materials, operations, labor, overhead and transport; Kearney describes combining product-level cost analysis with competitive bidding in PERLab's design-powered sourcing approach. [McKinsey should-cost paper · Kearney PERLab] |
| 3. Volume consolidation | Add eligible future demand for an approved common part across products and both plants—including separately identified service demand if the same contract can serve it. Test whether a joint RFQ, common specification, volume tier or fewer suppliers improves price and terms. My screening calculation: eligible future quantity × (current weighted comparable price − validated consolidated price) − transition costs. | Check logistics, tooling, qualification, minimum orders, inventory, lead time and resilience. Consolidation is not necessarily the right move if it creates an unacceptable single-source risk. Kearney identifies volume bundling as a procurement lever; its modularization work also emphasizes preserving customer-required performance. [Kearney Purchasing Chessboard · Kearney modularization] |
| 4. Duplicate and near-duplicate parts | Cluster plant-local IDs and supplier SKUs using manufacturer part numbers, drawings and technical attributes. Separate true duplicates—the same approved physical item under multiple codes—from near-duplicates that might be standardized through a design change. Quantify combined volume, price spread, inventory and administrative burden. | Do not merge lookalike parts on description alone: a seal's material, bearing's rating or component's certification may be decisive. Engineering and service must approve substitutions and spare compatibility. Sievo describes comparing suppliers and prices for harmonized materials and spotting duplicate records; its reported 6–23% material deduplication is a vendor-reported finding, not a savings percentage or a BOM-match-rate benchmark. [Sievo harmonization guide · Sievo direct procurement] |

Products are a lens on impact, not a separate price pool. If the same verified bearing goes into three machines, compare its purchasing prices once; then multiply a validated price improvement by the eligible total volume. Use each product's effective BOM quantity and build forecast to show which machines' costs would benefit. Do not claim the same supplier saving three times. Production-order issues are preferable to BOM demand when attributing historical consumption; the spend cube records purchases, which may still be in inventory.

### How much matching is enough to begin?

There is no public, generally accepted "BOM-to-spend match rate" threshold that I could substantiate, and I would not present one as a Kearney benchmark. Three commonly conflated measures need separate denominators:

- Spend categorization: can a transaction be assigned to a procurement category?
- Part identity match: can it be linked with confidence to a verified part?
- Product attribution: can its consumption be tied to this family's production, spares or warranty use?

A published McKinsey case reached 90% of spend categorized to taxonomy Level 4, above its 85% initial target. That is a real example of category coverage, not proof that 90% of invoice value matched BOM parts. [McKinsey case study] Sievo publishes 98%+ deepest-feasible taxonomy coverage and 94%+ classification accuracy as its best-in-class procurement-classification benchmarks; again, neither measures engineering-validated BOM linkage. [Sievo benchmark claims]

My working estimate, not an industry benchmark: I would start screening opportunities once roughly 70–80% of the in-scope, candidate direct-material transaction value has a defensible part match and the largest unmatched suppliers/lines have been inspected. I would not wait for 100%. But the gate for acting on an individual opportunity is stricter: validate the identity, specification, relevant volume, comparable prices and feasible alternative for that part. A 75% overall match is inadequate if the unmatched 25% contains the family's expensive drives or hydraulics; a lower overall rate may still permit a sound analysis of several fully validated high-value components. McKinsey likewise says manufacturers can begin with spend, descriptions, history and prices, then improve data iteratively where it will create value. [McKinsey on production-spend analysis]

I would publish value-weighted match coverage by plant and commodity, the unresolved-value queue, and each opportunity's status—price anomaly → technically validated → supplier-tested → finance-approved baseline → realized saving. That prevents an attractive analytical gap from being mistaken for money already saved.
