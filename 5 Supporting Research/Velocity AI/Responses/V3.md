For an industrial-machinery manufacturer, I would build a component-by-component business case, not multiply "parts eliminated" by a universal savings figure. Standardization can lower purchase prices and remove duplicated work, but a shared part can also over-specify smaller machines or require new tooling and qualification.

Evidence key: Public benchmark/example means a cited figure reported for a particular study, company, or population; it is not a guaranteed saving for this manufacturer. Estimate means a suggested screening assumption or calculation, to be replaced with client quotes, BOMs, inventory records, and labor data.

| Value or cost category | How to estimate it for a proposed common part | Range or anchor—and how to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Direct material: unit price and volume leverage | Compare annual as-built quantities × current landed prices with quantities × supplier-quoted common-part prices. Include freight, duty, scrap and any price premium from making the shared part capable of a higher duty. | No transferable percentage. For a screening sensitivity only, model 0%, 3%, and 8% reduction in the addressable purchased-part spend (my estimate, not a benchmark), then replace it with RFQs. ISM cites 2.5% procurement savings as a share of managed spend for industrial manufacturing in a 2014 cross-industry study; that is an organization-level result, not an expected discount on a standardized component. [ISM procurement-savings benchmark] |
| Direct material: design-to-cost / specification reduction | Price the redesigned common part or assembly against each displaced design; add or subtract material mass, manufacturing operations and purchased content. Keep this separate from a negotiated price discount on the same spend. | Kearney says modular design can reduce product costs by more than 20%, but this is an outcome claim for modular design, not a component-standardization assumption. Another PERLab case identified 12–15% direct-material COGS opportunity through broader teardown/design work, with engineering benefits from improved platform design; neither number should be applied automatically to a machinery BOM. [Kearney industrial modularization · Kearney PERLab design-driven EBIT] |
| Inventory and working capital | Forecast the change in average inventory dollars, including raw parts, WIP and service spares. Report the inventory reduction itself as cash released; separately estimate recurring holding cost as average inventory reduction × annual carrying-cost rate. Model pooled safety stock against demand variability and service levels rather than assuming every retired part's stock disappears. | 20–30% of average inventory value per year is a commonly cited carrying-cost planning range, but ISM explicitly says there is no consensus benchmark and that included cost components change the rate. Use the manufacturer's finance-approved rate when available. A $100k inventory reduction, for example, is $100k working capital released, plus perhaps $20k–$30k annual carrying-cost avoidance at that planning rate—not $120k–$130k annual P&L savings. [ISM on inventory carrying cost] |
| Tooling, fixtures, programming and setups | Count duplicate tools/fixtures that can actually be retired; annualize avoided replacement and maintenance. For future programs, compare one shared tool plus incremental capacity with several product-specific tools. Add changeover hours × loaded machine/labor cost only if schedules or capacity really change. | Quote-based, not percentage-based. I would use 0–100% of a specific duplicate tool's future replacement cost as a sensitivity (my estimate): zero when it must remain for service/capacity, full avoidance only when replacement is truly cancelled. Do not claim the undepreciated value of an existing tool as cash savings. Kearney describes faster assembly from pre-engineered modules, but publishes no general tooling-savings rate for machinery. [Kearney industrial modularization] |
| Engineering: new designs and continuing changes | For each avoided part or module variant, estimate avoided CAD/drawings, calculations, testing, release, ECOs, documentation and subsequent support hours × loaded hourly cost. Subtract the engineering required to create, qualify and govern the common design. | Avoided hours should come from project records, not a percent of headcount. As a single-company anchor, machinery maker BOMAG reported €1,200 internal cost for each newly created part in its system; that is not a universal fully loaded part-life-cycle cost. Count capacity released separately from cash savings unless budgets, contractors, overtime or future hiring actually fall. [BOMAG case reported by CADENAS] |
| Supplier qualification and management | Count supplier/part combinations eliminated, then value avoided RFQs, approvals, audits, contracts, performance reviews, quality investigations and supplier-master maintenance at the incremental hours or external fees actually avoided. | No defensible per-retired-part universal rate. ISM reports a historical 1.2% procurement operating cost as a share of spend for industrial manufacturing; it covers the whole procurement function, so do not multiply it by consolidated spend and call the result savings. It is context for checking whether modeled supplier-management savings are plausible. [ISM procurement-cost benchmark] |
| Purchasing transactions, receiving and inspection | Estimate purchase orders, receipts and inspections actually removed × avoidable cost per transaction. A smaller item master does not necessarily mean fewer transactions if purchasing cadence remains unchanged. | APQC reports a $150 median total "procure materials and services" cost per purchase order across its sample. That broad process measure includes more than merely typing a PO; it is an upper-context benchmark, not a marginal saving per PO eliminated. APQC separately describes roughly $14 to >$54 to process one PO, illustrating why scope matters. Use the client's marginal process cost and avoid counting the same buyer hours under supplier management. [APQC broad procurement measure · APQC PO-processing comparison] |
| Factory complexity, quality and service | Measure changed setup and pick times, line disruptions, inspection/test variants, rework, warranty claims and spare-parts stock. Value only the differences attributable to the proposed architecture; protect service interchangeability and traceability. | No portable percentage. A 2020 MIT study of Caterpillar axle-option proliferation found that adding operational inefficiencies plus internal and external quality increased its case-specific costing analysis by 64%. That is a strong reason to investigate omitted categories, not a 64% savings factor to apply elsewhere. [MIT/Caterpillar part-proliferation study] |
| Transition and ongoing platform costs | Subtract one-time redesign, interface work, prototype/test, certification, supplier requalification, new tools, BOM/ERP changes, old-stock write-offs and service-conversion costs. Add recurring platform governance and any higher-cost or heavier common part fitted to lower-duty machines. | Quote- and engineering-plan-based; no standard rate. This is the most important counterweight to gross "complexity savings." Kearney specifically cautions that standardizing for the most demanding condition can create over-specified designs. [Kearney industrial modularization] |

### A useful sanity check on "cost per part number"

Published figures vary dramatically because they count different things. BOMAG's €1,200 concerns its internal creation cost for a new part; a trade article citing Aberdeen research gives $4,500–$23,000 annually per new part number when sourcing, transactions and inventory are included. Do not add either blanket figure to the category savings above: it would likely count the same engineering, inventory and procurement benefits twice. Treat such numbers as prompts to ask what the client's incremental cost really is, not as an automatic bounty for deleting a part number. [BOMAG/CADENAS · Digital Engineering report citing Aberdeen]

My recommended business-case rule: calculate (1) recurring unit-cost and operating-cost changes, (2) one-time implementation cost, and (3) working capital released in separate columns, for each proposed common core or module. Use actual demand and supplier quotes for the high-value assemblies first. A component-count reduction is evidence of simpler architecture, but it is not itself a monetary saving: Kearney's heat-exchanger example reported over 60% fewer components and about 50% fewer system variants while preserving customer variety, without publishing a corresponding percentage saving for each cost category. [Kearney PERLab heat-exchanger example]
