For one industrial-machinery product family across two plants, I would build two linked views: a product view (which parts are in each machine and in what quantities) and a transaction view (what each plant bought, from whom, at what price). The bridge is a plant-aware part crosswalk, not a join on part number alone.

Source boundary: Kearney publicly describes PERLab's product-first work as analyzing components and rethinking how products are built and sourced. It does not publish a first-weeks BOM-to-spend procedure, a client extract specification, or staffing-day benchmarks that I could verify. The workflow, formats, priorities, and effort ranges below are my estimates/inference, not a claim about Kearney's internal playbook. ### What I would request from the client first

| Dataset | Requested line-level fields | Practical delivery format |
|---|---|---|
| BOMs, both plants | Parent and child part IDs, plant, product/configuration, BOM level, component quantity and unit, make/buy flag, revision, effectivity dates, approved alternates; ideally a flattened multilevel explosion and the original parent–child relationships. | Separate CSV or database extracts per ERP/PLM, with a data dictionary; XLSX acceptable for a small engineering-maintained exceptions list. |
| Item/part masters and cross-references | Each plant's item ID and description, manufacturer and manufacturer part number where available, supplier part number, drawing/revision, base and purchasing units, commodity, status, supersession and substitute IDs. | CSV/database extracts plus any maintained cross-reference tables. |
| PO and invoice lines, both plants | Source system, company/plant, document and line IDs, dates, item ID if populated, free-text description, supplier ID, supplier part number, quantity, unit, currency, unit price, net amount, PO-to-invoice reference, credits and line type. Request receipts too if available. | Line-level CSV/database extracts, rather than a pre-aggregated spend report; a separate existing spend-cube extract is useful as a control total. |
| Supplier master | Plant-local supplier IDs, legal/trading names, parent or group ID if maintained, status and location; purchasing-info/catalog records and part-number cross-references where available. | CSV/database extracts, one per source system. |
| Spare and warranty parts | Service-parts catalog and kit contents, service-to-production part cross-references, supersessions, machine model/serial applicability; warranty claim or work-order part-consumption lines, quantities, dates, plant and cost treatment. | CSV/database extracts; catalog or engineering spreadsheets for mappings not held in ERP. |

Those are recommended requests, not a published PERLab checklist. The field choices reflect how BOM records carry plant, validity, quantity and unit information, while purchasing and invoice records carry different item, document, quantity and price references. [SAP BOM documentation · SAP purchasing-item documentation · Oracle invoice-line documentation]

### First-weeks sequence — with effort ranked

All days are estimated hands-on analyst-days, not elapsed days. Activities overlap, and client IT, engineering, purchasing, service and finance time is excluded. Rank 1 is the most analyst-intensive in this messy two-ERP scenario.

Headers as pasted: Chronological step | What the team does; what makes the tie fail | Effort rank; estimate

| Chronological step (and what the team does) | Effort rank; estimate; what makes the tie fail |
|---|---|
| 1. Fix scope and accounting rules. Agree the machine models/configurations, both plants, BOM revision or build period, transaction period, and whether the answer is per-machine purchased-part cost, historical spend, or both. Define separate production, spare-parts sales, warranty and plant-maintenance buckets. | #7 · 1–2 days. Without this, an old BOM can be compared with current purchases, or the same bought part can be mistaken for the same use of that part. |
| 2. Receive extracts and profile coverage. Check row counts, unique document-line keys, date ranges, blank item IDs, missing PO references, currencies and units by plant. Compare extract totals with each plant's existing spend report; log missing tables and re-extracts immediately. | #6 · 2–4 days. Harder here: two ERP schemas must be interpreted separately; free-text purchases may have no usable item ID. |
| 3. Construct the product-side part list. Select the effective plant-specific BOMs, explode assemblies to purchased components without counting both a purchased assembly and all its children, and preserve quantity, configuration, revision and alternate-part rules. Keep spare kits and warranty replacement parts as separate usage paths. | #3 · 3–6 days. Harder here: plant variants, revisions, substitutions and service-only parts can make one apparently identical model resolve to different purchased parts. |
| 4. Build the transaction-side spend base. Standardize PO and invoice lines across ERPs; link invoices to PO lines where references exist; normalize signs, units, currency and suppliers. Select one basis for spend—typically invoiced net purchase value—and use PO lines to recover item detail, not as additional spend. Isolate freight, tax, tooling, services, interplant transfers and credits. | #2 · 4–8 days. Harder here: uncoded lines need description/supplier/PO context, and invoice-to-PO relationships may be one-to-many or missing. Oracle's documentation confirms that invoices and POs need not have a simple one-to-one relationship. [Oracle matching documentation] |
| 5. Resolve identities and join. First match within each plant on trusted ERP item IDs; then create a reviewed crosswalk between plant IDs, engineering IDs, manufacturer/supplier part numbers and service IDs. Use specifications, drawings, descriptions and supplier evidence for candidates—not automatic equivalence. Assign confidence and keep ambiguous/free-text lines unresolved until a buyer or engineer validates them. | #1 · 5–10 days. Hardest because of your assumptions: the same physical part can have two plant numbers; similar descriptions can mean different specifications; one supplier's number may cover a kit rather than a component. Public direct-procurement material likewise describes cross-ERP material/supplier harmonization and BOM-to-spend mapping as distinct data tasks. [Sievo] |
| 6. Reconcile product usage, spend and aftermarket. Calculate matched purchased-part price × BOM quantity for a representative build, then compare with actual invoice prices and purchasing volumes. Separately attribute spare and warranty transactions by service-part crosswalk and machine applicability; do not add all spare spend to the production BOM. Investigate unmatched high-value lines and differences in timing, inventory, scrap, alternate sourcing and purchase volumes. | #4 · 3–6 days. Harder here: purchases across two plants need not equal components consumed in machines during the same period; warranty replacements may use superseded or bundled service SKUs. |
| 7. Review exceptions and hand off a usable bridge. Have engineering approve equivalences, purchasing approve supplier/price interpretations, service approve spare/warranty mappings, and finance approve spend totals. Deliver a BOM-line-to-item-to-transaction bridge with source-system keys, match method, confidence, exclusions and unresolved-value totals. | #5 · 2–4 days. Harder here: more cross-plant and free-text exceptions require human decisions; a forced "100% match" would hide uncertainty. |

Total: approximately 20–40 analyst-days (estimate) for a focused first pass on one family. That is cumulative effort, not a promise that all records can be resolved in the first weeks. In practice I would prioritize high-value purchased components and high-value unmatched spend, publish matched versus unresolved coverage, and leave an auditable exception queue rather than invent part identities. The largest uncertainty in the estimate is the share of transaction value with neither a reliable item ID nor a recoverable PO/supplier-part reference.
